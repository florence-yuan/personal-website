I don’t know when it began, but for a long time now, we have been teetering beyond each other’s reach, our voices stretching feebly like fingers that struggle to touch; falling midway in their journeys, like birds that have lost the ability to fly.

These days we whisper; sometimes we shout.  When my mother wants to speak to my father she stands on her toes and shouts out into the too-thin air, and when he responds he has to crane his neck and work his voice up until his veins pop and his Adam’s apple shudders and moves like a lump of food he has not swallowed properly.

Every night they sit up until the clock strikes one.  I can see them now, huddled around the kitchen table, silent, my mother wiping her eyes and playing with the old necklace that goes around her neck, my father with his thin hair brushed over his ears, ending in a tuft that he keeps trying to flatten.  Their phones are turned up on the empty tablecloth.  There used to be a vase of flowers and a little hollow wood pigeon, and when I walked by I would fondle the flowers and take up the pigeon to blow.  Now the table is empty, and they wait in silence.  Occasionally a phone rings, and I hear my father and mother answering, their voices shaking with an eagerness I can hear all too plainly, “Yes?  Who is this?  Yes?”  And then within seconds that eagerness deflates, sinks, becomes the mumbled “goodbye”, and then back to the inaudible whispers, the sad murmurings that fall – ah, so frustratingly – into the lull of the night.

Sometimes I shout to them from the dark of my bedroom – “Oh god, why don’t you go to bed?  Half past twelve!  Go to bed!”  And I watch the square patch of light that seeps through my doorway, floating in the same shades as the weak tea that sits in their cups, drunk and refilled, again and again, never ceasing, never changing; only the hand that pours it trembles.  But the light does not move, and they do not answer.  “Go to bed!”  I scream, and wonder at their indifference.  Eventually my voice grows weak.  Sleep overtakes me and my eyelids droop shut, and I close my fingers on the last thing I happen to touch.

Once I woke up to find my old teddy in my grip; another time it was the wood pigeon; one morning it was a handful of dead leaves, fresh from the autumn splendor but now dry and crumbling into more pieces than I could hold.  Somehow the leaves sent through me a ripple of sadness.  I let them fall from my hands.

I saw a yellow note on the refrigerator; it had my mother’s handwriting, narrow and long and clean, letter separate from letter, little matchsticks standing apart: “3 Apples, 3 Oranges, place directly in fridge to keep fresh”.  I wondered if she meant to pick them up today.  On an impulse I tore the note from the fridge and stowed it in my pocket.

The day is fine and fair.  A swallow cuts the air, driving the slow, steady breeze, and I quicken my steps.  In the corner store I pick out three apples and three oranges.  The girl behind the counter looks at me rather longer than usual, but says nothing as she hands me the plastic bag with the fruit; I carefully hook the bag to my elbow and feel it swing against my arm, the fruit hard and glossy and supple and I wonder if they are red enough and fresh enough for my parents.  I remember my mother saying no, not fresh enough, sometimes through loud shouts and sometimes through writing; not fresh enough, my father said, I must buy again; their faces grew red with the shouting.  And I thought – what a waste of sound and paper, what a waste of the precious materials that you can only tear so many times from a tree bark, or from the particles in the air.  But I have been walking, on and on, my steps driven by the passing swallows, their tails fanned out and slicing the air.  On and on and on, where the street extends and appears to drop off at the horizon of that distant row of angled roofs, the horizon of my childhood that I have drawn more than a dozen times in these afternoons of leisure, the heavily demarcated roofs setting off a resplendent setting sun – red, dotted hard by the thickest marker I could find and breaking the paper with ink.  But when I reach its end the street does not drop off, and the houses keep extending beneath the pale wintry sky with the few clouds torn into so many pieces of feathers.  The last swallow careens and glides out of my sight.  I pull out an apple and hold it against the sky – the scarlet sun of the fairytale.

I stop when I am halfway across a bridge.  I lean against its railings and look down into the green waters of an unknown stream, and dimly in my consciousness I realize I do not know where I am.  I cannot hear my parents’ voices, the indistinct murmurings, the strained cries, echoes of my mother’s shouts – only the silence of the autumn day caressing the running stream under the bridge, under my feet.  I pull my mother’s note from my pocket and find it a crumpled heap, as though it has been there for a long time, like the dead leaves I held in my hand this morning.  I smooth it out and see the words, but not really reading; I trace the characters, the cleanness of the lines, the hasty dash of the comma that nevertheless stands straight, uptight, as though it would never fall.

I am lost, I stop to say.  I stop to say this halfway across a bridge.  I stop to look into the stream under the bridge, under my feet, where three wild ducks disappear into a clump of reeds.  I stop to see, and wonder if I’m really seeing.  I put my mother’s note back in my pocket and look hard at my reflection, and I wonder if it’s the water or the wind that makes my hair and clothes so unkempt, that dusts my whole body with scraps of falling leaves that weigh down, down, until they reach the final weight of the vivid, glossy fruits swaying in a plastic bag – the setting sun that I, the traveler, follow with despairing eyes.

I trace its trajectory into a black sea.  And I shiver; I say I am cold.  In the cold I feel the silence, the emptiness; in the cold I see the yellow lights of my own house, now in a black cluster beyond my horizon, on the other side of the sun; in the cold I see my parents around their empty table with their phones turned up to catch echoes of this nether world of which I am one.

Over the darkening waters I cry out to them.  I see my cry torn into the leaves that litter the world.  Too far, too far, they become flightless birds that drop into seas and oceans.  I see my mother stroking my father’s hand.  I see them trying to speak, but the words fall away in the frigid, merciless air.  Then the wind heightens, the sky swirls, and the water rises, and it pulls away at my pockets, pulls away the note, the teddy bear, the pigeon, the dead leaves, and tugs hard at my hand holding the fruit; but I will not let go, and against the dark current of water I see the apples and oranges floating up like round orbs of light, and in my mind I see my mother’s note spelling out in letters that stand straight like matchstick soldiers, standing tall and will never fall: “Keep fresh, keep fresh.  Directly.  Keep fresh…”  And yes, that’s it I think, I must return or the fruit will go bad, I must go, I must listen, I must bring back the fruit.

And then the water falls and I hear an answering cry in the lull of the tide, and I cry back and our voices touch like wings, and I see your phones lighting up but you no longer notice, and I come into your embrace like the first time I drew the sun blazing over darkened roofs, only now I have crossed the horizon and I bring back the fruit from the other side.
