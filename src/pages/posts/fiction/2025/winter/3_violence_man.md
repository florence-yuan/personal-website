The rubber man swayed softly at the other end of the attic, its arms outstretched, stretched too wide for an embrace.  Stephen stared at it, bile rising in his throat.  Its whitish, amorphous visage faced him indifferently, soft and impalpable and featureless, looming now and then as it tilted backward and forward and was thrown into the light.

Stephen closed his eyes, clenching his fists.  He felt the pressure of Connolly’s fingers on his shoulder.  “Ready?” came Connolly’s whisper.  The blue-eyed man with the soft brown mustache and the enigmatic glasses, with a white card that said ‘DR G. H. CONNOLLY’.

Stephen swallowed.  He nodded, and then he found himself remembering how it had all begun, in a dingy street on the wrong side of little blithe Court Avenue, and a mile away from the latter’s orderly white pavement and trimmed chestnuts.

Two days ago he had seen the advertisement in dingy Miller Lane, plastered inside the display window of a derelict building that had once been a paint shop.  The building had two stories and the attic windows were very small and curious, like tiny caves.  The shop sign on the ground floor had been hacked off, leaving behind a block of grayish cement.  The new sign was yet to be put on.  Through the panes of empty glass beside the advertisement, Stephen could see rusty buckets standing around on the paint-splattered floor.

The ad alone looked new.  It was made of a shiny, lurid green material, its edges curling off from the glass.  Large black letters stated:

THE V ROOM.

WILL Open 9:00 AM – 18:00 PM

Ever felt the need to unleash your inner beast?

Ever felt the need to pummel back at the modern world, i.e., the true beast that oppresses and squeezes out the vital energy within?

Ever felt the futility of smashing beer bottles and pounding your wall?

Here’s your solution!

Visit THE V ROOM and enjoy the true, unfettered experience of violence release, purging the demon within and pummeling that demon without!  Arrange for an early consultation or visit on our opening day.

A telephone number and a date followed, and calculating numbly, Stephen found it to be the day after the next.  For a few minutes he lingered in front of the ad, twisting his fingers in his pockets and licking his lips.  The lurid green burned itself into his eyelids and made him giddy and weak.  He tried to collect himself, but instead felt the soft, gray substance that was his soul writhing like a pot of putty.  Yes, his soul was soft and gray and tasteless.  He had attempted to sculpt it, but had always found it too mild and too yielding, too easily falling into water.  The words from the advertisement danced in front of his eyes, and he thought with a mocking scoff: “Beer bottles!  Pounding your wall!  As if the modern man can be so violent!”

And he wasn’t.  Wasn’t violent at all.  He was a mild, mild man.  He had tried to scream, once, in his office, after everyone else had left and he crouched alone at his desk under the straight, flat light.  He had tried to scream – had felt the echoes of a scream in his ears, behind his eyes, tingling in his lips.  It had torn itself from him like a bird fleeing fire, then crumpled before it had even begun.  Whimpers.  He had whimpered on his desk, whimpering like a wounded child.  He couldn’t scream.  He was too mild, too mild.

He walked on, passing the shop.  It was mean and shabby, sure, but there was something in the jumbling of the acid green background and the black letters, the bad letters all skewed and curled and jumping out at him, grabbing him – that had struck its destination.  He thought of the blocks of cement that was the interiors, and shuddered.  It was too gray, too like his soul.  Then he remembered the curiously glowing windows that had peered out above the broken sign, the windows peering curiously out at him from under a red gabled roof.

He wondered if it was all right – that attic.  He wondered if he had seen a burst of acid green within it.  He wondered if the burst of acid green could come from his own soul.

He stood still in the middle of the road.  Between a street sign and a sloping roof he could see the orderly chestnuts and street lamps of Court Avenue, floating in a bluish mist that was the sky.

And suddenly, a car honked, a man shouted, “Hey, get out of the way!  Whatcha doin’?”

He stepped backward, swiftly, silently, averting his face as the car sped past and sent up a whirl of dust.

Gray matter, a lump of his soul snowing, falling, staining the tip of his nose and the tip of his shoes.  His phone lighted up in his pocket and began to ring.  It was Hector calling.  Hector at the office with his large red face and large red forefinger, bellowing at him at the top of his voice.  Delays, deadlines, mistakes, a report to be rewritten, a phone call made badly, a lunch break overstayed.  A horn blaring, a thousand cars passing, rattling the windows of his fragile room that rose among all the other rooms like an insect standing on tenuous legs.  He could no longer kid himself.  This was it – the modern animal.

A traffic light jumped from red to green, the legs of the stick man began to move, and Stephen walked forward, head down, shoulders bent, into the city of the towering insects.

The rubber man swayed, and the long length of the attic narrowed into a single shaft of light; the rubber man was a white moth fluttering against the light.

“Are you ready?” came Connolly’s voice, soft and like butter melting.
And again the pressure of his fingers, again the impression of his pale blue eyes in the shadows.

Connolly had said in their consultation that all men were beasts, and the V-Room was purposely based in a ‘wild place’ to attract these alienated souls.

“Why so ashamed of violence?” his mild, gentle voice had said, “Why make it taboo, when it is its release that we crave?  Why can’t it be like something else – virtue, liberty, freedom, these things most men shamelessly and openly pursue?  Why not say – it is also beauty, it is also light, only that it’s different.  It is the purging of shadows.  Isn’t that also light?”

And it was light, thought Stephen.  It was the light of a moth struggling against fire, or a fire struggling against a moth – but which was it?
From the shadows came Connolly’s voice – gentle, deep, reassuring.  “If you are ready now, Mr. Hedling, I’ll snap on the headset.”

“Yes, of course.”  Stephen felt the words leave his lips like a swift, faint breath, an exhalation.

The pressure was removed from his shoulders.  He heard Connolly working behind him, then the headset was snapped over his eyes; in an instant all lights were put out, and he saw nothing and heard nothing apart from the pounding of his own heart.

He was floating in a room of darkness.  A single light, suspended midair and he knew not how far away, cast a shivering ring of luminescence.  It blinked and flashed like a firefly’s tail.  It was held by a hand.  It illuminated the hand.  It was a human hand.

The firefly flashed and the hand that held it glowed like a strange, luminous crab.  It was an old hand, scabbed and marked with age.
The firefly flashed.

Stephen moved closer, his eyes transfixed on the firefly and the hand.
And slowly, sliding like a snake, the hand that had hitherto cupped the firefly removed itself, and the light streamed upward, brilliant like a torch.  Now he could see a human face, and never a more repulsive, ugly face had he seen – it was sallow, sagging, and harshly lined around a pair of bloodshot eyes whose color was no longer discernible.  It was a face that was animal in its brutality, and yes – it was brutal.  It bared its teeth and flared its nostrils; it was cruel, and invited cruelty.  Grayish soot smeared its face and drool dripped from its teeth; but it was not its animal traits that made it grotesque – it was the human things, the livid eyes, the shrunken gums, the withered cheeks, all these things that were all too real, all too piteous and pathetic.

And Stephen cowered.  He could not stand this image of human pathos	; he could not stand the sight of its suffering, its repulsive ugliness.  It seemed to mock his senses and taunt him too cruelly.  He moaned and half-closed his eyes.  He could not bear it.

Then the face glitched, dissolved into little particles of pixels that flashed and swirled and reassembled into the amorphous face of the rubber man, the pliant, featureless face that bobbed through the light and shadows.  His stomach dropped; the whitish face swayed in his vision, cold and machine-like.  Then it became the grotesque face again, switched in an instant of pixel fireworks, and the mouth snarled and bared its yellow teeth, and suddenly the yellow scabbed hand seized a ball of fire and hurled it toward him; he yelped as the fire made contact with his skin, and though it did not hurt, he could see a wound burned into his hand.  Shivering, he looked up – the ugly face was smiling, but only for an instant; another swirl of pixels, and it was back to the rubber man, cold and white, impassive in a sheet of flat, industrial light that came from the attic ceiling.  “It is all fake,” murmured Stephen to himself,  “All set up to fool me.”  Then the awful possibility came to his mind that the grotesque face existed only in his imagination, that he had hallucinated it – yes, that was it, hallucinated it in his moment of weakness.
But he must not be weak.  In his weakness he had let his mind take over his soul again.  He tried to remember Connolly’s instructions, “Get ready to do whatever you like, Mr. Hedling.  Yes, anything you like.  Sock the man, slap him, knock him down – he’ll stand up again, or not, if you so wish it.  It’s all in your mind, sir.  You know what you want.  Do as you like.  Just get the fists ready.”

Get the fists ready, thought Stephen.  Cold sweat trickled down his face, but he gritted his teeth and clenched his fists.

The rubber man swayed closer.  He was ready; he tasted the salt of his sweat on his lips…  Smack!  His fist caught the left side of the rubber face, but the moment it landed he let out a scream – the rubber man had changed back into the grotesque old man, and the cheek he had struck was bleeding, bleeding from the pores of the skin and from the lips of the mouth, the angrily quivering mouth that murmured to itself and smacked its shrunken gums, and the blood that poured out became a ball of fire, that the old man, with a hair-raising snarl that chilled the air, launched toward Stephen…

And he began to run; he turned and ran, not looking back, not stopping till he felt a hand grasp his shoulder, and he heard Connolly’s voice, all gentle and soothing and buttery, “What is it, Mr. Hedling?  Is there a problem?  Do you want to stop now?”

And Stephen crumpled.  His knees went weak and he slumped forward, and he whispered, almost whimpering, “Yes, yes…  I want to stop now, Connolly.  Yes, please let it… let it stop now.”

He felt Connolly reach around his head to unfasten the headset, and he was so weak he could barely speak, but he moaned, now and then, to himself or to Connolly he did not know, “Hurry up, hurry up, make it stop…”
Then he heard a faint click and the headset was lifted from his head.  For a few moments, Stephen took deep gasps of air, looking frantically around; yes, he was still in the attic, but there were more things than he remembered – worn tables and cupboards and a wall littered with holes and cracks, and the rubber man was still swaying in a corner.  Nauseated, he turned away from it.

And Connolly was grasping his arm; Connolly was speaking soothingly, sympathetically, “Are you all right, Mr. Hedling?  If you would need any assistance, any help of any kind…”

But Stephen did not hear him, and when he breathed more freely he said, “What…  what was I supposed to see…”

“Mr. Hedling?”

“I mean what was I supposed to see after the headset was on.  The room, the rubber man, the…”  He meant to say the “grotesque creature with the human eyes and animal mouth”, but something made him pause, and he looked suddenly at Connolly.  The man’s polite, soft-angled face was still carefully arranged into an expression of sympathy, but in his pale blue eyes Stephen thought he read a hint of mirth, even contempt.
“No, it’s nothing, I…”  Stephen broke off.  What was he supposed to do?  Admit that he was weak?  Explain his hallucinations and round it off as a joke?  He averted his eyes and shook his head.  “It’s nothing, Connolly, I’ll just go.”

“Are you sure you don’t need help then, Mr. Hedling?  Anxiety, nerves…  I quite understand.  Perhaps I should inform the manager…  Discuss some rest and therapy…”

“No, no, Connolly.  There’s no need for that.  I’m all right, really.  Goodbye.”

Stephen stuck out his hand to shake Connolly’s, and then he walked out through the door.  Yes, everything had been transfigured, all placed back in their proper location.  The attic was back to a slick long corridor with doors opening into rooms, and the door that swung closed behind him held the plaque “ANGER RELEASE”.  It was all in his head, and Connolly would never recommend him to think of violence as the counterpart to virtue and liberty, or to equate light with the purging of darkness.  No, Connolly would prescribe a glass of water and a few pills; therapy, if he needed it, in a small room that had no windows with the strained-looking therapist who kept rubbing his eyes and drinking coffee.

There came a shout of laughter from behind, and Stephen turned to see another door in the corridor burst open.  He recognized a colleague of his, Lafferty, laughing as he emerged from the doorway, dragging what looked like a crumpled mummy.  As Lafferty hauled it out inch by inch, Stephen saw that it was another rubber man, but so pummeled that it had deflated, a large crack splitting its side.  “Hey, can’t I keep this, doc?” Lafferty was saying while he laughed, “Looks a real good trophy, it does…”

And then Connolly emerged, and though he shook his head he was smiling, shaking his head and smiling like an indulgent teacher who was overseeing the successful graduation of his most mischievous student.  “Now, now, Mr. Lafferty, you don’t want to do that, though I bet you went all in when he launched his fire ball, eh?”

“Sure, doc.  Just had to beat the bastard up!  I must say, the new virtual experience is a great bonus, all those effects, just amazing.”
“And that must be the fifth dummy you’ve gone through in ten days, Mr. Lafferty.  Mr. Hector won’t be too pleased to see another one pinned up to the moodboard, so just leave it in the box.”

“Ah, that was a joke, Connolly, I swear!  Just a joke!  And the funniest thing…  Remember that Hedling, that great wimp of a fellow?  He saw my rubber dummy that morning and got scared out of his wits, went all screaming and crazy and just tickled everyone with his hysterics!  I swear, even Hector was laughing…”

Stephen heard Connolly say something mildly reproving, but he kept walking till he was out of earshot, his forehead burning and his hands very cold.  He found himself next to a window, and glanced out of it.  Rows and rows of glass panes stared back at him, and everywhere were the tidy white letters on the dark mauve background spelling out “Court Avenue”, and everywhere the orderly chestnuts rustled and sent up the ding of the city.


It was midway through lunch break, and hunched over his table in the office, Stephen was watching the quivering mosquito.
The mosquito was trapped in an empty jam jar; he had managed to trap it by bringing down the jar suddenly and quietly, with only a flex of the fingers and a quick whoosh of air.  But the mosquito was still alive.  It buzzed lazily in the glass jar, bouncing off the sides.
Stephen observed the mosquito closely; its long black legs were slender and insubstantial, like bent toothpicks dotted with segments of white.  He heard a gentle voice from his youth, “Observe it, Stephen.  Don’t touch it; let it be, but observe it.  See its fuzzy legs and the spread and quiver of its wings.  Observe it, Stephen.  Take note.”
He had been looking at a dragonfly.  Now he was looking at a mosquito.  He wondered if a mosquito could be equally beautiful as a dragonfly.  But then his mind recoiled and said, no, how can a mosquito be beautiful?  And that was it – the dragonfly was beautiful, but the mosquito took blood, and was ugly.

Take blood.  “Never take blood, Stephen.”  The dragonfly hummed and took off from the blade of grass, leaving it bent and swaying in a golden fuzz of sunlight.

The mosquito bounced off the walls of the glass jar.  But it did not take blood.  And Stephen would not take blood.
The mosquito moved its slender legs and landed on the bottom of the jar.  Its legs were beautiful and insubstantial.  Stephen’s soul was beautiful and insubstantial, perched on top of twenty-six stories of glass panes and cement ceilings.

The mosquito was perched on top of the glass bottom of a glass jar.
Glass rattling.  A hand smacked the table, and Stephen looked up to see Hector standing over him.

“Looking at a mosquito now, Hedling?” Hector sneered, and his eyes were cold.  “Got it in a jar, prizing it?  Dawdling over it?  Perhaps you have not understood, but in this company we prize a certain degree of force.  You don’t understand?”  He moved his hand to the jar.  “Force of personality, force of decision, force of action.  Ring a bell?”
Stephen nodded mutely.

“Yes – lines we should all do well to remember.  And by that motto,” Hector seized the jar, “We move to action.  And when we see a mosquito –”  His fingers tightened over the glass jar, tightened over the fragile glass of a building that rose thirty stories into the sun, with a mosquito quivering at its bottom.  “We kill it.”

He flipped the jar over and, in an instant brought his hand down on the mosquito, and Stephen felt his own breath stutter.

Slowly, without moving the heavy square fingers, Hector turned up his palm.  The mosquito with the blood.

The man bleeding in a setting sun.  Hector smiled, and wiped his palm on Stephen’s table, smearing it with a streak of red.  And then his face loomed close, his eyes flashed, and his mouth leered, showing pearly white teeth, “Wimp.”

And suddenly the blood-bloated body of the mosquito burst, and the sun flooded the thirty stories of the glass skyscraper, and the brilliant light went up, up, and became the ball of wrath.

And Stephen launched himself at Hector, launched his own feeble limbs at the large bulk of Hector’s square head and square nose and square fists, knocked his own fists into Hector’s face, knocking them into him until Hector fell with an ugly yell, and bruises were blossoming on his face and blood streaming from his nose and mouth…

And Stephen was standing with triumph, his eyes wet with an unfamiliar emotion, his mouth twitching with the beginnings of a smile.

He had triumphed.  He stepped back, and looked grimly at Hector.

He had triumphed.

And then the pixels glitched.  A firework of pixels, Hector’s ugly, bruised, bleeding face glitching into a flat, white face without features, without eyes.  Hector again, and then the face of the rubber man was back, and it swung toward Stephen from the shadows into the light, waiting to be hit.

“Sock the man, slap him, knock him down – he’ll stand up again, or not, if you so wish it.  It’s all in your mind, sir…”

Stephen backed away, mouth open in mute horror.

And Connolly’s voice came from behind him, “Well, that’s it, Mr. Hedling.  Shall I take off your headset now, or would you prefer another round?”
