The advertisement said everybody was welcome to apply. As long as you were over fifteen,
and you had the soul of a poet. How quaint and funny, thought Eliza Reynolds with a smile,
to be talking of a poet’s “soul”; or was it bad humor? But something within her was stirred by
the flamboyant words – “join us, whether as a performer or spectator, in our annual
celebration of that divine thing men call poetry, where emotion finds thought and thought
finds words.”
It was the announcement of a poetry competition that she was reading, where the
applicants would recite their own poetry to a panel of judges, and the next week the results
would be published in the newspaper, and the winners would get awards, a slip of paper and
some cash. Eliza smoothed the page over and felt another little quiver of excitement. She had
poems, dozens of them, dashed down in fits of fervency, in these transient passages of time
when she had felt the suppressed tremblings of passion (or was it dreams?) She did not know
if her poems were good; oh, liar, she thought they were good, but hated it when people
examined them and gave hesitating, lukewarm comments. She never could muster up the
courage to send them to print, not even to the school newspaper. Her parents had urged her,
many times, but sending her poems to some newspaper, to an editor, always seemed too
audacious a leap, too personal an effort between two people who were wholly apathetic, and
yet were linked by a sheet of paper one sent and the other was obliged to read, a poem she
thought merely tepid and not even good enough.
But a poetry competition – that seemed different. It was like middle school days, when you
were awarded prizes based on merit or academics or some event, and those who got the
prizes stood in lines facing the teachers who were really bored to death, and were checking
their watches ever minute or so, but judged the hundreds of you as an amalgamation of duty,
to be repeated year after year. That was what appealed to Eliza now. To be one of hundreds,
simultaneously, so if you did badly it was not your fault, and it was nothing personal, and you
would be forgotten quite soon, instead of, say, joked about over tea the next day when the
pathetic little editor who read your poem laughed about it with a colleague.
Oh, she would love to apply, thought Eliza, dropping the newspaper abruptly and clasping
her pale cheeks with her hands. To read your poetry to some acknowledged specialist in
literature and to perhaps be liked, and – and – to be something! How important that was for
her, who couldn’t even carry on with half the classes she took, because she thought them
uninteresting and dull, and now always with too much leisure on hand, half of which she
spent dashing down lines, the other half carefully stashing them away, so that no one would
see them, and they could lie in a dark corner, dreary and unfinished. Yes, she was old enough
to court reality, only in her heart she still felt like a child. She was scared of the dark. She
treasured her dolls as much as her books. She could not hold a conversation with a stranger.
Nor with friends now, it seemed. Joey had moved away the previous year, and they never
talked now; he was busy working weekends in a mechanics shop. And Maisie – well, Maisiewas popular now! The thought struck Eliza queerly, and she gave a small, startled laugh.
When did that come about? she wondered. It seemed that all of a sudden Maisie was in the
debate club, on the school rowing team, had many new friends, and wore such fashionable
clothes. Another thing she herself was deficient in, Eliza thought with a sigh. She had no
dress sense, and the most recent time she had managed to meet Maisie – in the cafe just round
the corner, because Maisie liked slick, adult things now – Maisie had looked shocked at her
dress. “Is my dress so very bad?” Eliza had said in desperation, in a last-ditch effort to make
conversation. “I thought it’s just flowery. I hope it doesn’t embarrass you, Maisie. I would hate
to do that.” There had been an awkward silence after that, and then Maisie had shrugged,
“No – no, it’s all right.” But she couldn’t hide her grimace, and Eliza noticed suddenly that
her lips were painted bright red.
But here was chance. Something real. I wonder if they will like me, thought Eliza as she
dragged her nails over the table and winced at the scratching noise, like cat claws. Oh, but
you’ve got to give it a try, you don’t know until you’ve tried. She went on listlessly in her
mind, then stopped when she realized she had been saying what her parents had been saying
to her, what everybody had been saying to her, for years and years. But she would give it a
try, she decided, standing up. Read out her poem to these people, like a real poet would, a
real Bohemian poet who could sleep in the streets as long as the walls around him were full of
art. Oh, she could be a Bohemian poet if she wanted, Eliza thought with sudden fierceness.
She could curl up under balconies of other people, as long as they occasionally dropped a
flower on her head, and allowed her to make poetry. She could, of course she could. She
stood up; when the chair checked her movement she bent down and noticed the small note at
the end of the advertisement, stating that entrance fee for those out of state was fifty dollars.
God, how awful, thought Eliza, growing miserable again as she moved to the kitchen to pour
herself tea. How awful that modern society was all about the transactions and the currency.
The poor poet gets nothing, she thought, and drank very weak tea without sugar.
But it was all right. She would ask her parents. She knew them well enough to know they
would do anything for her to advance her dreams. She would ask them, maybe even ask for a
couple hundred, because this time she meant to go alone (the “why” she did not condescend
to dwell on). Anyway the competition was in the neighboring state, and would require taking
the train, and it ended at five so she would probably have to stay at an inn. Oh, inns were
always wonderful, thought Eliza as she imagined the inns she had read in novels, with the
rich broths bubbling in a corner while strangers told their stories around ancient oaken tables.
Some wild adventure always ends at an inn. So there’s anticipation at the end of a long day,
that at night the adventure still continues, and lingers on until dawn comes and smashes
everything up.
Light, splintering white light, thought Eliza, setting down her tea and looking absently out
the window, where the autumn night was still snug and dark, and the drone of automobiles
faded into low wails against the sound of leaves rustling and the odd dog barking, the street
lights dim and heatless and succumbing to the cooling weather, and the loneliness of thestreets curling round the corner like mist. Abruptly the feeling broke over her that out in the
night there was nothing, no light and no sound and no warm comfort. Another hour and the
yellow lights of the cafe would be put out, and the shop fronts would be like blank dark
canyons with no eyes, and all that was human, that could be anchors to grasp onto, would fall
away into the susurration of the night.
A cold breeze flitted into the room, and she gave a little shiver. No, she could not possibly
tell her parents tonight, nor tomorrow morning; not when they had just returned from their
visit to a great-aunt she had dreaded meeting. The very idea of human contact brought closer
to her that amorphous fear that so often consumed her, and she did not know its parts – was it
her great aunt, was it the act of asking for money, was it herself? No, she would put it off. She
would not think of it just now. Instead she would look through her poems and pick out some
good ones. So she went to find them, and several minutes later laid them down in a great
ragged heap on the table. Frowning, and with tired fingers, she set herself to the task of
looking through the very works she had tossed away, and was embarrassed anew by the very
things that had made her discard them – some discordant note, perhaps, or a trace of naivety,
a tentativeness she did not like. When finally she had secured a few that did not jar her
senses, it was eleven o’clock. But there was one she thought she might actually read aloud. It
was about a walk in a rain, but not really. It was actually a little about flowers, a little about
emotions, and a little about life. Not the passionate kind, where everything begins and ends
in violent moments of the senses. No; for some reason flowers were never that to her; they
were softer, gentler, tamer, marking and being marked by a weeping soul who imprinted them
on her flesh to make fragrance, and then imprinted death on them by plucking them from
their stems and wounding their delicate petals; they were dabs of an artist’s brush that could
be like folds of clouds that spread upon an evening sky, chased asunder by the faintest breeze.
She might read that, thought Eliza, yawning a little now that the time was getting late. It
was a nice poem with delicate touches, white and misty and ethereal, and strewn with the
pinkish gray petals that grew yellow with age. But she would decide the next day; not now.
The night was deepening, and her eyes felt tired. Tomorrow afternoon she would talk to her
parents, she thought, and look up which train she would have to take. But not now, not now.
In the silence the ticking of the clock was loud, and jarring. She stood up and lifted the
discarded poems back into their box. She would go to bed now, she thought, and smoothed a
hand over the newspaper before casually tossing it into a drawer. It, with its little black letters
and startling prose, belonged to the daytime glare. Somewhere out in the night she heard a
car roaring. Instinctively she looked out the window, where the trees hovered and swayed
and confused the shadows. And then she saw a band of white light streaming through the
darkness, bright and searching and turning round, directed at her, almost.
But it couldn’t be. And it was already fading, blurring and losing focus and fading with the
guttural groans of the car. The road loosening and giving way, and the tires going over the
flattened land where shreds of moonlight lay with eyes heavy with sleep. And she turned
around and told herself she would go to bed soon, and worry about everything tomorrow, yestomorrow, she promised herself rather wistfully, as she stood for a moment hesitant by the
window, luminous with its half-brewed enigma. And then she stooped and put out the light.
Mr. and Mrs. Reynolds were easily convinced. Of course they would supply the money, if
Eliza needed it. Her great-aunt, incidentally, had also sent her money, as a treat for her
upcoming birthday.
That evening, holding the envelope with the money in her hand, Eliza was strangely
deflated. She felt chilled, even a trifle irritated. How the adults delighted in making money,
ugly money as physical as possible! She might have had a credit card, but no, it was paper
money she was given, crisp, physical, neatly tucked into a white envelope and rustling
whenever she shook it. But how absurd she was! How absurd, how unreasonable, when she
ought to be grateful! Groaning, Eliza placed the envelope into the drawer with the newspaper,
carefully locked it, and then buried her face in her hands. Oh, but she was going! Finally,
clawing through the air not with claws but with lax fingers she had gotten something. She
was going, and she had better prepare her poem. To be frank she had already been preparing,
and was almost able to recite it, if only she were not so nervous. She stuttered when she was
nervous. She must not do that. Her great-aunt and her parents had given her five hundred
dollars.
Over the next week she went over her poem in her head again and again. The coming of
each morning brought a renewed dread, and the first thing her sleep-deprived mind
remembered was a line she had forgotten while reciting last night, when she had fallen asleep
in an agonizing fit of anxiety. Even at school she kept reciting it. Classes dragged on, more
inconsequential than ever, but flew past when she began to repeat her poem under her breath.
Once she bumped into Maisie, who on another day she would have been anxious to talk to,
but now could not be bothered to say more than a vague “hello”. Time itself grew surreal.
Morning seemed still recent when evening wafted through her bedroom window and
darkened the room, and scarcely had the moon shone when it was put out by the new sun’s
brilliance. As a consequence the hours seemed no longer to be of importance, or even to be
distinct, but all gathered into a conglomeration of confused light and sound, that rushed by in
stuttered instants, and would only be quiet deep into the night, when midnight crawled into
bleary-eyed dawn, accompanied by cup after cup of weakening tea.
How do you read a poem? Passionately, temperately, or evenly? She hated her own voice
when she heard it over and again. In time she hated her poem also, and wanted above all to
twist its words with little tweezers and squeeze some sentiment out of them that she could
hold onto, that she could make mellow with her own insipid reading.
Oh, agony! And the week flew past, and the last cups of tea were consumed, and there was
only water, weak and almost transparent, floating limply in her cup. The dreaded Saturday
came. It was not quite morning yet, for it was only half past five, and dawn was just breakingin unresolved shades of pale gray and amethyst. But it was time to leave. She said goodbye to
her parents, and with trembling hands she packed her things and her poem, because she
could not relinquish the solid words in ink even though she knew them by heart.
When she stepped out of the apartment door she was pale and feverish, and the moment
she breathed in the cool autumn air she felt her own cocoon shattering, and then the world
was upon her. She saw that the day was fair, and for the first time in many days she was
impressed by the blue of the October sky, and stopped for a moment to take in its splendor. In
hours everything would be over, she knew, and the next morning she would see the blue
again. But somehow the picture of now, of the yet-unknown and undecided, a blue untainted
and pure and innocently unaware, moved her and made her eyes grow moist.
She was on the train. It went steadily and blithely forward, and outside were hills and
towns, alternating, and quite beautiful in the muted morning sunlight. But she could not
think of that. All she could think of was the ocean of unknown faces at the competition, the
feeling of being jostled in the crowd and in the sunlight, and then to stand, unsupported and
with violently trembling knees, in front of all the faces blurred together and the sounds
humming loud and low, and you see only the faces of the judges, and notice their irritation,
their displeasure, their severity. Oh, her knees were trembling even now, and sweat made her
hands slip and fumble as they extracted the fragile sheet of poetry from her little bag, and wet
with blurry marks the already crumpled paper. The rain, the flowers, the walk. The person. The
intensity of the sunlight was making it difficult to read, but when she tried to edge toward the
shadows the woman sitting beside her was irked, and said rather loudly, “Do you mind?” No,
Eliza did not mind, of course she didn’t. She was meek and apologetic; she stowed the poem
back into her bag, and sat with her cheeks burning and hands folded for the rest of the
journey, every inch a timid schoolgirl. Finally the train pulled into the station, and she left
with a feeling of relief, until she remembered it was eight-thirty and she must hurry, and
anxiety pulsed hard and fast through her veins and made her breathless.
When she arrived at the venue it was already crowded, and she paid the entry fee to go in.
It was an open area, with a large lawn at the back where the spectators and contestants could
sit, and a carpeted stage in front. The white of faces and clothes were sharp in the uninhibited
sunlight. Grass crunched under people’s feet, and a drone of voices and laughter filled the air.
A man with a megaphone was shouting that the applicants should register themselves first if
they wanted to perform in the morning round. Of course, there would be two rounds, only
Eliza hadn’t thought about it, and now she had to decide if she wanted to do morning or
afternoon. The prudent thing was to do morning, and then all would be over, and she
wouldn’t even have to find lodging. But I must be prepared first, thought Eliza, fingering her
bag anxiously, and it doesn’t do to hurry. No, I will sit and watch everyone else first, and do it
in the afternoon. Now the man was shouting for everyone to move along, so she hurried with
the crowd and went to sit in the audience, in a plastic white chair with legs that creaked and
wobbled. She was glad when no one moved to talk to her. She preferred solitude. She took
out her poem again and lay it over her knees, and tried to remember and think.Meanwhile the event began. A finely dressed woman walked onstage, introduced the
judges, and announced the commencement of the annual poetry reading competition. The
rain, the flowers, the walk, thought Eliza, trying to straighten the paper. The woman in the rain.
The first contestant was reciting his poem. It was a patriotic poem, and it was quite stirring
the way he read it, with wide gestures and a passionate eloquence. But she wasn’t interested;
she was more interested in the judges, as everyone seemed to be. She could just see their back;
she couldn’t see them clearly. There seemed to be two men and two women. All of them
seemed to have gray hair, and Eliza sat back, rather disappointed. Older people must prefer
patriotism, she thought morosely, some grand thing playing out like drama. They shall hate
my poem. How insignificant my poem is! A little walk in a little rain, and flowers bruising
under a person’s touch. Just the sort of a thing a schoolgirl would write. Who shall want to
hear me read it? And she jiggled her knees and read it over again, and the words blurred
where the sunlight and the sweat of her hands left their mark.
On and off went the contestants, some reading better than others and receiving louder
applause. They talked of nature, the country, the world. When they were insipid Eliza felt
slightly better, when they were eloquent decidedly worse. And it was all over again in the
afternoon. People reading one after the other, only the spectators were steadily dwindling,
and in her plastic white chair Eliza felt more alone. She ought to be registering now, she
thought, she ought to be. There was still time. It was four o’clock, no, a quarter past. The sun
was slanting west, and the shadows were lengthening, even here where there was nothing to
block the sun, even here, the grass colored more unevenly and the plastic chairs more
insubstantial, everything emptier. She thought there was irritation in the air, and the judges
were no doubt tired too, hardly in the mood for a little poem that told of nothing but a
meandering walk, and the meandering of a mind in a single afternoon. Oh, how foolish she
would seem if she read it now! Why not sooner, then, you fool! she cried inwardly. Why not
sooner, and everything would have been over, and you would have been happy now. But it
was not over, and the searing heat of feverish, unrealized dreams scorched her eyes and made
her tremble. Her hands sweated profusely as she gripped her poem. She read over it again
and again, went over it in her mind, imagined herself to be on the stage, staring down into an
emptying audience with little white chairs and a yellowing sun. Abruptly she wished she had
been just a little more daring in days gone by, done something outrageous even, like walking
onto the polished stage of the school theater and performing some poem or play, performing
with passion and ardor, and letting all the pent-up joy and tears overflow, and spill over in the
bright and dizzying lights. Why not, why not, ever? When she had been younger she might
have said, because I’m not a performer. Now she knew that it was not merely about the
performance, not to a bright roaring crowd anyway, but about having a soul and baring it, to
other souls you did not know, souls in plastic seats on a shimmering lawn under the intensity
of the sun.
But she couldn’t do it. With a sudden lucidity and something of relief she realized it. She
just couldn’t. She couldn’t walk on stage and read her poem. She could not brave lightsartificial or natural, or shout out her fears and laughter to the swaying, lilting forms of other
souls in the diluted striations of the waning sun. She was too weak and too afraid. Perhaps
she had not meant to do it from the start, thought Eliza, from the very beginning when she
sketched out the whole plan, and envisioned herself taking on an adventure. But it was no
adventure. In a dim part of her mind she saw the faces of her parents, her friends, beckoning
at her, some smiling, some not, all of them making gestures at her and saying words, words
she could not hear. Her hands shook more violently, and with wide dry eyes she looked up to
see the event still happening, and she realized that she was still here, in the present, trapped
to her seat and forced to watch it happen, knowing she could not escape it just as clearly as
she knew that the inevitable end was coming, as the sun slanted west and colored the sky
with faint wisps of red.
The classically dressed lady was on the stage again. Time was running out now, she said, if
there were still applicants who wanted to read their poems they should hurry up. Hurry up,
hurry up now. The stragglers looked excited, the stragglers sitting in groups, circular rings of
chairs they had pulled close together. They liked conclusions, even these without decisions.
They finished the last of their snacks, dusted the crumbs off their shirts. Soon all there would
be left would be the ravaged remains of the aftermath. A glaring red sun in the west
overwhelming the green of the lawn, and the rickety plastic chairs sitting around in
disorganized groups, and the crumbs and plastic cups scattered in the grass. And squeezing
her eyes shut Eliza felt the agony in her being, the agony of the long wait and its end. Just do
it, she whispered to herself, do it now, stand up and walk up there and do it. She was out of
breath; she was almost panting. She clutched at the piece of paper and heard it tear, slightly.
The flowers and the rain and the walk, and the person, all alone. And see the pink-gray petals
falling. Strewn. My being. All of my being, she said, and felt tears gather in her eyes. All of
my being, and I can not do it.
And then it was all over. The judges stood up, shook hands and bid goodbye to each other
and left, and the stragglers, still talking excitedly among themselves, trailed out of the venue
in loose groups. And Eliza stood up too, her legs numb from sitting and her hands very cold.
It is all over, she told herself. And look at you, you – you coward. She could not bring herself
to look back as she walked into the streets. She knew what she would see if she turned,
something like a giant skeleton, white and sprawling and already dead, past life and even past
decay. She walked into the streets. She did not know them. And how desolate they were,
how lonely and desolate, the street lamps not yet lit and the roads gray and glistening with
the pallor of a dying sun. The stragglers from the competition turned down an alley and were
gone. If I had read my poem this would have been just like an adventure, she told herself.
Something to explore and to reward myself with. But now it’s not mine; none of it is mine. I
am a coward, and I am all alone, and I deserve nothing. Oh, but what’s the use of it all? I
ought not to think of it. Nothing would have come of it anyway, nothing but superficial
delight. And nothing, nothing matters in the end. Why must I always be superficial? Why,
always? As she thought of this, violently and passionately, she clutched the sheet of poetryclose to herself, close to her heart, and tried to hear, in the evening lull, the soft sound of
pattering rain and falling petals, and the murmuring of a woman who belonged to her
imagination and yet was distinct, a woman stronger and more fortunate, for she was a woman
who lived in poetry.
