I came upon Teresa’s story by chance, when a friend lent me an old newspaper with the headline “Counter-revolutionary disrupts public order”, accompanied by a blurry black-and-white photo of a young woman on a rooftop, holding up what appears to be a blank sheet of paper – though that might be owing to the temptation of drawing a connection to the recent “White Paper Protests” in Shanghai.  The strangeness of the woman’s stance intrigued me, and in my subsequent investigations I was struck by the parallels – and differences – between Teresa and her namesake, Saint Teresa of Avila, which drove me to assemble the brief biography below.

Teresa was born in provincial Shanghai in 1945.  Her father was a landowner, and a few short years after the end of the Chinese Civil War he would be crucified on the cross of capitalism, never to see freedom again.  Miles away, nine-year-old Teresa would see their house torn down by an angry mob bearing hammers and torches, while she shuddered with helpless tears at her mother’s side.

Teresa was twelve when she had her first vision.  In a rapidly splintering world she was drawn to a cluster of white words that appeared like a slogan or a sign, and it was more beautiful than anything she had seen.  Teresa knew no god and no deity – she had been raised with none.  But from this moment a god emerged, and it was all white text and no objects, because in objects little men with hammers and torches were born, and in objects fathers disappeared and houses were torn down like pictures.

In this abstract blaze of passion Teresa grew, often malnourished but with a faith that worked steadily – some might say insidiously – on her starved, eager imagination.  She took up writing, and dashed down poems on yellowed sheets of exercise paper; she hoarded books that were not about the great leader, not about communism, not about the weapons and tools that lifted up a new party and tore down her earliest home.  These were dangerous texts, folded into old newspapers and old books and packed at the bottom of cardboard boxes; Teresa’s uncle collected them.  He was the reedy, sallow type, what Teresa’s mother contemptuously called “a useless intellectual”; but he allowed Teresa into his attic, and it was there that she first read the translated works of the French Modernist poets, Baudelaire and Rimbaud, and their words in her mind began to paint an image of the world that bled into a bitter disillusionment – their carcasses and death became her disdain for flesh, their musical language a vessel for a dream-like realm where poetry and nature reigned supreme.

When she was fifteen, a local scuffle broke out between the Mao loyalists and dissenters.  Flags were waved and granaries broken into, and the streets filled up and emptied.  Teresa’s mother screamed for Heaven’s mercy and hoarded their food rations at the bottom of the cupboard.  Teresa struggled to write – the world was so ugly, so full of noise, and she had terrible headaches that only later they figured out were signs of epilepsy.  She refused food in the hope that she would shed her mortal skin forever, and in moments of delirium she scrawled unintelligible words in the margins of her textbooks.  Then in a bout of hunger she yielded and broke her fast, having written no great book or epic poem; thus ended her failed martyrdom.
Three years later, she was visited by the local party committee, to recruit her into the Down to the Countryside Movement.  They invited themselves into the house and sat for hours a day in her mother’s parlor, alternating between threats of punishment and pontificating about the necessity of re-education and manual labor for youths.  Secretly, Teresa was amused; had they not taken away her childhood, she might have been swayed by their words.  After all, her soul had always yearned for the ideal.  But she no longer believed them; she believed in books.  Her mother was less certain.  “Teresa,” she said, “It’s necessary that you go, don’t you see?  Would you rather they took your brothers and sisters?”  And beside her stood Teresa’s younger siblings and half-siblings, the oldest fourteen, the youngest in swaddling clothes.  That night Teresa packed up her things and left for her uncle’s house.  It was as the French said – love had to be reinvented; Teresa wasn’t sure if she believed in anything unconditional.

Her uncle was poor, lukewarm, and rather antiquated in his views, but when it came to intellectual opposition he was all for it.  He was the one who nudged her into entering a correspondence with editors of the old literary newspapers.  A few were kind in their replies, and that was how Teresa found herself drifting about in the canteens of universities she did not attend, eating little and feeding solely on conversations and writing.  It was there that she found her dreams taken seriously, there that she made her first experiments in surrealism, seizing on the shades of unreality she’d first discovered in French poetry.  Then the Cultural Revolution began.
This time the streets filled up and never emptied.  Violence, violence of a magnitude she’d never seen before, surged into a madness that seemed surreal, like a nightmare gone out of hand.  Objects, too many objects, jostled each other – scarlet armbands, endless copies of Mao’s Little Red Book, and the black “capitalists’ dunce caps” that were pinned on teachers, scholars, anyone suspected of being counterrevolutionary, in public trials that went on daily.  Nothing made sense.  Teresa was helpless; how were books and poems going to temper insanity and cruelty?  Some of the editors who had been kind to her committed suicide by jumping into lakes, and others she’d known were being harassed and humiliated by a frenzied mob.  Teresa no longer starved herself; it seemed almost heartless to inflict such minor cruelties on herself when everyone else was suffering so acutely.

And then one autumn day, the news came that her brother was dead.  He had been sent in her place to a rural province, had labored for two years, and then an ox cart ran over him and killed him.  Like a fool and a coward she had run away, searching for an ideal, and her brother had been made to take her place, and now he was dead.  To poor Teresa, the whole world seemed part of a concerted effort to mock her and wound her.  Hadn’t she said she cared nothing for objects and people alike?  Now they slipped out of her helpless, terrified grasp, and her ideals were not nearly enough.

What happened next I shall draw from the newspaper clipping mentioned in the beginning of this story.  A year had passed since her brother’s death when one day, the locals were shocked to see Teresa – who had not been seen in the neighborhood for years – appear on the rooftop of the local school building.  It was such a strange sight, and she wore such a strange mix of reds and blacks, that she seemed to come from a dream.  With arms spread apart, she held up a large sheet of paper that bore thousands of minuscule characters, some flowing like lines of poetry, some into large symbols for peace.  She said nothing, only stood for half an hour, and when they finally climbed up to escort her down she went willingly.  No one could agree on exactly what was written on the paper; according to all accounts it had been burned, so it might have been blank after all.  Was it a dream?  Perhaps it was, but everyone remembered it like they remembered a startling work of art.
Teresa’s punishment was as public as her silent protest.  They made her sit in a chair on a makeshift platform in the town square, and a megaphone was pointed at her ear, playing on repeat the party’s mantra.  In the cacophony she felt her world splinter into colors and shapes, and she was nine, twelve, and fifteen years old again, half within this world and half outside it, with her soul malleable to the touch of god.  Later, onlookers would say she’d had an epileptic fit, but to Teresa she had had her final vision, and this time the symbol was not a white object but the lines she had written herself, the poems she knew would find some eternity when she’d conferred them, with the bittersweet weight of foreknowledge, to her uncle’s care, and which I would find on a dusty shelf in a private archive.  Three days later she was dead.
In another time, in another life, Teresa’s visions might have been transfigured into a different kind of ecstasy, and her idealism might have found fruit in concrete reforms.  As it is she was merely a mortal who tried to make a beautiful cocoon for herself, and failed, and it was by leaning into the absurdity of the world that she left her greatest mark upon it.  Which is better?  I do not know.  Perhaps she was born too late, perhaps she was born just at the right time.  Perhaps it does not matter, because this Teresa has always inspired me to keep reflecting on ourselves and on this age, to follow our ideals but also our hearts, and most of all, to never lose sight of our human compassion.

I hold Teresa’s story very dear for some other reasons also.  It was inspired by the lives of my grandparents and great-grandparents, who lived through the Chinese Civil War and the Cultural Revolution, and whose lives are testament to the fact that surrealism has never been just an aesthetic.
