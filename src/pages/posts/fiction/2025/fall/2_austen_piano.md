Of the staples in Jane Austen’s fictional drawing rooms, the pianoforte ranks as one of the most richly symbolic. It is perhaps no coincidence that all of Austen’s heroines (with the exception of Fanny Price and Catherine Morland) are accomplished piano players, but none of them is a consummate artist. A collector of sheet music herself, Austen played the piano mainly for the amusement of her family – an object of entertainment, not artistic aspirations. In her novels, then, there is less interest in the skill itself than in the inextricable link between performance and personality – art not for art’s sake at its finest. In Pride and Prejudice and Emma, musical performances are much more than they appear; for in a time and age where women are often silenced, the female performers’ manipulation of the pianoforte demonstrates their taste (and distaste) of musical conventions, an acceptance or rejection of spoken and unspoken rules. In Pride and Prejudice, Elizabeth Bennet makes lively conversation with Mr. Darcy over the piano, which becomes a vehicle and backdrop for the expression of the female mind. In Emma, however, the piano is a more ambiguous, painful symbol, and elicits a less optimistic view of implicit gender roles.

In Pride and Prejudice, the pianoforte appears several times in early scenes, and we learn that Elizabeth, while a capable performer, does not play “half so well” (PP 22) as her sister Mary. The significance of the instrument comes to the fore at an evening party at Rosings. Everyone begins to discuss music, and after Elizabeth speaks with “spirit and flow” on the subject, Lady Catherine gives a condescending, long-winded lecture about the importance of piano practice. Austen crafts Lady Catherine as a comic antithesis of Elizabeth. She is both haughty and ill-bred (eliciting a look of shame from Mr. Darcy), both well-versed in society and ill-versed in its subtleties. Her views of music contrast sharply with Elizabeth’s natural spontaneity, and her comment that “no excellence in music is to be acquired, without practice” (PP 168) represents the voice of polite society. The musical education of a young lady in the 19th century was therefore supposed to be harmonizing – to temper her impatience, to cultivate character, and ultimately to anchor her to the drawing room. More than all else, however, music – which Lady Catherine declares “of all subjects my delight” (PP 167) – was a mark of good breeding. Indeed, she makes a point of discussing her own exceptional instinct, stating that “there are few people in England… who have more true enjoyment of music than myself, or a better natural taste” (PP 167). Taste, then, was indicative of an even greater virtue than discipline. If discipline at the piano was merely middle class, taste – simultaneously nebulous and lofty-sounding – was interpreted as a mark of true aristocratic character, one that Lady Catherine only deigns to bestow upon herself and her own daughter.

While Lady Catherine exalts the virtues of taste and discipline, Elizabeth turns a humorous eye upon both and dismisses them with easy levity. As usual, however, she uses humor to disguise nonchalance – accusing Darcy of trying to “frighten” her, and stating that “I will not be alarmed though your sister does play so well” (PP 168). In one stroke, she acknowledges her inferior skills and displays casual indifference, or as Elizabeth puts it, “a stubbornness about me that never can bear to be frightened at the will of others” (PP 168). This stubbornness carries into a half-humorous, half-serious effort to deride Mr. Darcy for his aloofness. Obviously attracted by Elizabeth and not knowing her real dislike for him, Darcy states that he lacks “the talent… of conversing easily with those I have never seen before” (PP 170). This provokes Elizabeth to make a comparison between socializing and playing the piano, implying that just as she should attribute her own inferior skills at the piano to a lack of practice, Darcy should attribute his own lack of social prowess to his unwillingness to be friendly. While Elizabeth intends this comparison to wound, it is clear to both Darcy and the reader that their shared indifference for most people’s opinions is precisely a great similarity between the two. As Darcy puts it, “We neither of us perform to strangers” (PP 170). Indeed, for all the tone of self deprecation in “not [taking] the trouble of practising”, Elizabeth is totally indifferent to her own lack of musical proficiency. She plays the piano to fulfill her basic responsibilities, to entertain, and to avoid conversation when she so chooses (especially with Lady Catherine). Her casual attitude is plainly demonstrated by her ease at playing and then stopping to make conversation; the instrument is the perfect basis for her metaphor targeting Darcy, and in turn the perfect metaphor for her social ease and self-sufficiency. In all its capacity as a “feminine” instrument designed to cultivate discipline and to attract men, the pianoforte fails to appeal to Elizabeth. And unlike her moralizing sister Mary, who takes all the fun out of her performance by playing long, difficult pieces, Elizabeth plays with mediocre talent but a great deal of pleasure, and so gives pleasure to the social setting she inhabits. In this way, she deftly manipulates the pianoforte to suit her needs – entertaining when she must and giving pleasure when she can. And despite the veneer of propriety, the piano is precisely an instrument of self-assertion for Elizabeth, bolstering her statement about her courage and stubbornness.

An instrument of female assertion in Pride and Prejudice, the pianoforte has more complicated undertones in Emma, and musical performance more ambiguous symbolisms. During an evening party at the Coles, Emma Woodhouse learns that Jane Fairfax has received a piano – “a very elegant-looking instrument… a large-size square piano-forte” (E 278). At first glance, the piano appears to be a wonderful, thoughtful gift. Jane is proclaimed to “play… delightfully” (E 279), but did not own a piano before due to her lack of wealth. Indeed, everyone agrees that the piano must be a great consolation to Jane, a channel through which she can express her artistic sensibilities, an instrument to expand her narrow existence in genteel poverty. Austen does not let us indulge in the benefits for long, however, for immediately the heroine, Emma, begins pondering the more mysterious aspects of the gift – its sender and the reason for anonymity. She jumps at the conclusion that the piano is a gift from Mr. Dixon, and her active imagination readily supplies an account of a illicit romance between Jane and Mr. Dixon. In Emma: Jane Austen’s Errant Heroine, Eugene Goodheart says, “Her ‘imaginism’ in its willful reality-bending predilection is the antithesis of the romantic sympathetic imagination” (Goodheart 597). Armed with a healthy dose of willfulness, curiosity, and the insatiable desire to be both insightful and right, Emma falls down another rabbit hole of incorrect guessing. And since the reader necessarily views the event through Emma’s eyes, the pianoforte begins to take on more mysterious hues of repressed sexual desires, quite the opposite of being “romantic” and “sympathetic”. By making us share in Emma’s idle speculation, Austen enlarges the symbolism of the piano, while also making us a “guilty” party in participating in sordid fancies. However, as Goodheart states, Emma’s errors and failures lead to true discoveries of affairs of the heart. Just as eventually Emma learns to sympathize with Jane, we too are reminded of the necessity of compassion. As we go on this journey of discovery and self-reflection with Emma, the piano serves as an anchor of both reality and fancy. It stands for the contrast between Emma’s fancies of mysterious affairs and the truth, with the former enhancing the pain of the latter. The truth of secret relationships is much more painful than what we imagine, Austen reminds us, for it is necessarily built on secrecy and oppression, a closed box that the piano perfectly represents. Indeed, the piano is the only way through which Jane can express her feelings to Frank, and music the only medium where they can collaborate.

It is fitting that in a novel about real and imagined affairs of the heart, Austen should employ a recurring symbol of love. The pianoforte, however, does not only mark the passions and sorrows of a secret relationship; more than that, it is a symbol of the oppression of women. The distinction between the rights of men and women is prominent in the party at the Coles. Despite also being in a secret relationship, Frank’s behavior is diametrically opposite to Jane’s. While she is forced to be reserved and quiet, he flirts shamelessly with Emma, even joining in on her speculations about the origin of the mysterious piano. While Jane is flustered by the reception of the piano, Frank is almost giddy in his pride about selecting and sending it. While she must suffer in silence, he can still enjoy himself in the ballrooms of high society. Music is the channel for their connection, but at what price? Ultimately, the woman is still oppressed, her voice conditional and granted by the man, her path to a difficult romance narrow and quiet. True, Frank does eventually repent, explaining his casual flirtation with Emma away as a necessary disguise. But this disguise cannot but feel too convenient, his levity too inconsiderate and boisterous in the face of Jane’s quiet suffering. And even the gift of the pianoforte seems to be more an exercise in vanity, a surprise for surprise’s sake and a self-assurance of his good intentions, than a genuine gesture of love and consideration. As Mr. Knightley remarks, “Surprises are foolish things. The pleasure is not enhanced, and the inconvenience is often considerable” (E 296). Wealth and status, too, play a part in this oppression. Frank, though not wealthy, is well off; and Emma, who is one of the wealthiest characters and still very much immature at this stage of the novel, can sit pleasantly by and speculate on Jane’s love life, if only to satisfy her own curiosity. Jane, on the other hand, is in straitened circumstances and lives with only spinsters for company. As Laura Vorachek states in The Instrument of the Century, “In raising the specter of female sexual desire with the piano, Austen's text illuminates the economic and sexual constraints that middle-class ideology places on women” (Vorachek 34). Being relatively poor and a woman, Jane Fairfax must necessarily suffer in her quest for love and marriage.

For both Elizabeth Bennet and Emma Woodhouse, the piano is a social necessity, a demonstration of taste and education, as well as an instrument for pleasure. As such, they play it to fulfill their social obligations. Art and music do not pose great attractions to the true heroines in Austen’s novels; indeed, those who do excel at the instrument – namely Mary and Jane – are depicted as solemn and slightly lackluster figures, inferior in some significant way to the heroine and thus forced to channel their creative energies through the pianoforte. Elizabeth and Emma, however, are the indisputable superiors in each scene; and if Elizabeth is not exactly as affluent as Emma, she still lives in a state of comfort, with little to worry about and less to prove about herself. Their wit and charm are best employed in human relationships, not introspection or artistic development. The pianoforte, then, serves to set apart these two vivacious heroines from their peers and remind us that their energies, their fancies, and their passions are directed into neither art nor artifice, but into love and intimacy, the driving force of Austen’s novels.  If there is a lack of poets, artists, and writers in Austen’s novels, it is not only owing to the necessity for realism, but also to the ultimate triumph of love over discipline, and imagination over rationality.

Like other human inventions that become staples of polite society, what the piano represents is a combination of social rules and the personality of its practitioner. When the former overwhelms the latter, we are reminded of the disproportionate shackles society places on women, as in the case of Jane Fairfax. Yet when the character herself is in control, then ease and vivacity come to the fore, and unforgettably lively characters such as Emma and Elizabeth emerge. Through the musical abilities of her characters, Austen constructs a vivid world of diverse personalities. Love, too, is a consistent theme. The scene at the pianoforte precedes Darcy’s first proposal, and marks an important step in Elizabeth and Darcy’s mutual understanding. The theme of love is a more fragile, painful one in Emma, more easily misinterpreted and speaking to the tragic implications of being a genteel woman in poverty. For Jane and Frank, the piano is an emblem of forbidden love and suppressed desires, with the woman who plays at it powerless, and the instrument itself subject to the man’s whims. Ultimately, Austen’s novels do not rebel against class and hierarchy. As Vorachek notes, “…the piano provides authors and artists of the nineteenth century a means of representing female sexual desire without overturning, and in some cases even endorsing, middle-class constructions of women's sexuality” (Vorachek 39). Nevertheless, through Emma and especially through Elizabeth, Austen reminds us that our choices do define our lives, and the way her heroines choose to express themselves on the pianoforte indeed raises their voice, expressing values of autonomy, independence and true love that shine bright in spite of the constraints of 19th century English society.
